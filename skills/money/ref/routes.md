# The two routes, in full

Read when working steps 3 and 4. Each route is a way of turning a thing people
want into revenue. They are not opposites and a product can run both, but one
of them usually pays the rent.

## Direct: the buyer pays for access

| lever | what it meters | where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| seats | people with a login | teams share one login; growth stops when headcount does |
| metered usage | calls, rows, minutes, gigabytes | the bill is unpredictable, so buyers cap usage and stop growing |
| gated features | a capability the free tier lacks | gate the wrong thing and the free tier stops being useful enough to adopt |
| tiers | a named bundle at a named price | tiers drift into a matrix nobody can read, and the sales call becomes an explanation |
| enterprise SLA | uptime, response time, indemnity | it is a promise with staff behind it; price it as headcount, not as a checkbox |
| switching cost | data, integrations, trained habits | earned lock-in is a moat, imposed lock-in is a reason to leave at renewal |

**The two questions this route lives on.** What is the first paid step, and why
would anyone take the second? A model with no answer to the second is a
one-time sale wearing a subscription's clothes.

**Free tier.** It is not charity and it is not a trial. It is the cheapest way
to find the buyer, and it works only when it is genuinely useful at its own
size. A free tier crippled to force the upgrade teaches people the paid one is
crippled too.

## Indirect: access is free, the surroundings are sold

| lever | what is sold | what has to be true |
|---|---|---|
| open core | a proprietary layer over a free base | the split has to be legible, or every roadmap fight becomes a licensing fight |
| managed hosting | not running it yourself | running it must be genuinely annoying, and you must be better at it than they are |
| take rate | a cut of what flows through | there has to be flow: transactions, listings, jobs |
| support and integration | your people's time | it scales with headcount, not with users — margin stays flat |
| certification, training | proof someone can use it | needs enough people employed on it to make the proof worth having |
| a free standard | nothing, directly | you devalue a rival's paid entry and earn the right to shape the format |

**The question this route lives on.** What does scale earn here that charging
for access would not? If the answer is "more users", it is not an answer:
users who never pay and never bring a payer are a cost. Real answers are
network liquidity, a data position, a format everyone builds against, or a
support relationship nobody wants to switch.

## Choosing

Ask what the thing's shape already decides:

- **Who bears the pain of running it?** They bear it → managed hosting has a
  buyer. You bear it → charge for access.
- **Does anything flow through it?** Yes → a take rate is available and usually
  beats a licence. No → there is nothing to take a cut of.
- **Would a competitor's entry get cheaper or dearer if this were free?**
  Cheaper → giving it away funds them. Dearer → the free standard is a weapon.
- **Can a buyer tell, before paying, what they get?** No → tiers and gates will
  not sell it, and you are looking at support or hosting.

## What this file will not do

It gives no numbers. Acquisition cost, lifetime value, conversion and take rate
are properties of a specific market with specific customers, and nothing in a
repository settles them. Where the answer needs one, the lens asks for it or
hands back the question (G5).
